\title{Geostatistics as hyperparameters, and design-based interval
coverage, for digital soil mapping}

\author[uf]{Hugo Rodrigues}
\ead{rodrigues.machado.hugo@gmail.com}

\address[uf]{University of Florida, Range Cattle Research and Education
Center, 3401 Experiment Station, Ona, FL 33865, USA}

\begin{abstract}
Map accuracy must be estimated from a probability sample by design-based
inference rather than by spatial cross-validation. That established result
concerns point accuracy. Map uncertainty, the prediction interval attached to
each mapped value, matters just as much for decision making, yet its coverage
is almost never checked against the sampling design. This paper makes two
contributions of comparable weight. The first concerns where geostatistical
decisions are made. Digital soil mapping has largely displaced geostatistics
with machine learning, and where geostatistics survives it usually enters by
assumption: a variogram family chosen by habit, a fixed lag binning, a range
consumed as a constant. We instead place those choices in the hyperparameter
space of the learner, where they are selected out of fold against a null in
which no correction is applied. The variogram family, its range as a working
scale, the lag binning and estimator, the anisotropic geometry, the reach of a
spatial gate and the strength of the correction are all selected rather than
assumed. We are explicit that geostatistics here supplies the hypothesis and
feature space rather than the final estimator: the selected corrector is
usually a residual forest whose features are variogram-scaled basis functions,
and ordinary kriging wins outright in only a small minority of repetitions.
The estimator built on this rule, DB-TARF, lowers the design-based root mean
squared error relative to the default random forest on the Amazonian
above-ground biomass data of Wadoux et al. (2021) under all three sampling
designs (random 32.49 to 31.62, regular grid 32.28 to 31.40, clustered 35.78 to
34.54 \Mgha{}), winning 28 of 30, 30 of 30 and 29 of 30 paired repetitions. The
second contribution extends design-based assessment from accuracy to interval
coverage. Coverage is a population proportion, so the same Horvitz-Thompson
argument that makes the accuracy estimator design-unbiased applies unchanged.
Under representative sampling the cross-fitted conformal intervals are
calibrated and roughly 17 per cent narrower than a quantile regression forest
baseline; under clustered sampling plain conformal under-covers, a failure only
the design-based assessment reveals, and a spatial-block conformal calibration
restores validity.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

\citet{wadoux2021} established that the statistically valid route to point map
accuracy is probability sampling with design-based inference, and that spatial
cross-validation is biased and unsuitable for validating a map. Two questions
follow. Given the correct yardstick, can the map be improved? And can its
uncertainty be validated under the same principle? The second is the more open:
prediction intervals in digital soil mapping usually come from kriging
variances or quantile heuristics, and their coverage is rarely checked against a
probability sample. An interval method can look reasonable and still under-cover
the population.

This paper makes two contributions of comparable weight.

\paragraph{Geostatistics as hyperparameters} Digital soil mapping has largely
migrated from geostatistics to machine learning \citep{mcbratney2003}, and where
geostatistics survives it usually enters by assumption: the analyst decides
residuals should be kriged, picks a variogram family by eye, fixes a binning and
an estimator, and adds the correction whether or not it helps. We propose that
these decisions belong where every other modelling decision belongs, in the
hyperparameter space of the learner, validated out of fold and competing against
a null in which no correction applies.

Two clarifications about what this does and does not claim. The individual
ingredients are established: regression kriging \citep{hengl2018} and stacked
generalization or the super learner \citep{wolpert1992,vanderlaan2007}. What is
new is that the geostatistical decisions are made on evidence rather than by
convention. More importantly, geostatistics here supplies the hypothesis and
feature space, not the final estimator. The corrector that the data selects is,
in the large majority of repetitions, a residual random forest, one whose
features include radial basis functions scaled by the fitted variogram and
whose output passes through a variogram-calibrated spatial gate
(Section~\ref{sec:choices}). Ordinary kriging wins outright in a small minority
of cases. The claim is therefore not that kriging has returned to the centre of
the map, but that where, at what scale, in which geometry and with how much
confidence a spatial correction acts are geostatistical questions, and that we
let the data answer them.

\paragraph{Uncertainty under the design-based paradigm} We define and estimate
prediction-interval coverage as a design-based population parameter, extending
\citet{wadoux2021} from root mean squared error to coverage; we give the
estimator conformal prediction intervals computed from its own cross-fitted
residuals; and we show that plain conformal calibration is well calibrated and
sharp under representative sampling but under-covers under clustered sampling,
and that a spatial-block conformal calibration restores design-based validity.
To our knowledge this is the first design-based assessment and correction of
prediction-interval coverage for large-scale digital soil mapping.

The two contributions are connected. The same cross-fitting that selects the
geostatistical hyperparameters is what supplies leakage-free conformity scores
for the intervals.

\section{Design-based assessment of point and interval accuracy}
\label{sec:designbased}

\subsection{The population, the map, and where the randomness lives}

Let the domain be a finite population of $N$ cells, $U=\{1,\dots,N\}$, with true
values $z(s_i)$, and let the map be a function $\hat z: U \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$.
Once produced, the map is fixed and so is the true field; the only randomness
admitted is that of the validation sampling design. Nothing is assumed about
autocorrelation, stationarity or the model that made the map
\citep{degruijter2006,brus2011}. That is what makes the assessment
assumption-free: a model-based assessment is only as good as the process it
posits, whereas a design-based one inherits validity from a randomisation the
analyst controls.

\subsection{Any cell-level loss is a population mean}
\label{sec:ht}

Let $g: U \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$ be any function evaluated cell by cell, and
let its population mean be

\[
\bar G = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i \in U} g(i).
\eqnum{1}
\]

Draw a probability sample $S \subset U$ of size $n$ with inclusion
probabilities $\pi_i = \Pr(i \in S)$. The Horvitz-Thompson estimator
\citep{horvitz1952} is

\[
\widehat{\bar G} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i \in S} \frac{g(i)}{\pi_i}.
\eqnum{2}
\]

It is design-unbiased for $\bar G$, and the proof is one line. Writing
$I_i = \mathbf{1}\{i \in S\}$ for the sample-membership indicator, we have
$\mathbb{E}[I_i] = \pi_i$ over repeated sampling, so

\[
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\widehat{\bar G}\right]
= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i \in U} \frac{\mathbb{E}[I_i]}{\pi_i} g(i)
= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i \in U} g(i) = \bar G .
\eqnum{3}
\]

Under simple random sampling without replacement $\pi_i = n/N$ for every cell,
and Eq.~(2) collapses to the sample mean,

\[
\widehat{\bar G} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i \in S} g(i).
\eqnum{4}
\]

Everything that follows in this section is a corollary of choosing $g$. That is
the whole point, and it is why extending the framework from accuracy to
interval coverage costs nothing conceptually.

\subsection{Point accuracy: $g$ is the squared error}

Taking $g(i) = (\hat z(s_i) - z(s_i))^2$ gives the population mean squared
error and, by Eq.~(4), its design-unbiased estimator,

\[
\mathrm{MSE} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i \in U}\big(\hat z(s_i)-z(s_i)\big)^2,
\qquad
\widehat{\mathrm{MSE}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i \in S}\big(\hat z(s_i)-z(s_i)\big)^2 ,
\eqnum{5}
\]

with $\widehat{\mathrm{RMSE}} = \sqrt{\widehat{\mathrm{MSE}}}$. One caveat
deserves to be stated rather than hidden. Because the square root is concave,
Jensen's inequality gives
$\mathbb{E}[\widehat{\mathrm{RMSE}}] \le \mathrm{RMSE}$, so the root mean
squared error is not exactly design-unbiased even though the mean squared error
is. The bias is of order $n^{-1}$ and is negligible at the sample sizes used
here, but it is a property of the square root and not of the design. Taking
$g(i) = \hat z(s_i) - z(s_i)$ gives the mean error, which is exactly
design-unbiased. We also report the squared Pearson correlation and the
model efficiency coefficient of \citet{nash1970}, following the reference
study.

\subsection{Interval accuracy: $g$ is the coverage indicator}
\label{sec:picp}

Let $[\hat \ell(s), \hat u(s)]$ be a nominal $1-\alpha$ prediction interval
attached to every cell, and choose

\[
g(i) = \mathbf{1}\{\hat \ell(s_i) \le z(s_i) \le \hat u(s_i)\}.
\eqnum{6}
\]

Its population mean is the prediction-interval coverage probability, and
Section~\ref{sec:ht} immediately supplies a design-unbiased estimator,

\[
\mathrm{PICP} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i \in U} g(i),
\qquad
\widehat{\mathrm{PICP}} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i \in S} g(i).
\eqnum{7}
\]

Here $g$ is an indicator, so $\widehat{\mathrm{PICP}}$ is a sample proportion.
It is exactly unbiased, with no Jensen problem, its design variance under
simple random sampling is $\mathrm{PICP}(1-\mathrm{PICP})/n$ up to the
finite-population correction, and confidence statements about it are
elementary.

This is the conceptual contribution of the paper, and it is deliberately
unglamorous: coverage is just another population mean, so it inherits the
entire design-based apparatus that \citet{wadoux2021} established for accuracy.
A valid interval has $\widehat{\mathrm{PICP}} \approx 1-\alpha$, and among
valid intervals the better one is narrower, measured by the mean prediction
interval width

\[
\mathrm{MPIW} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i \in S}\big(\hat u(s_i) - \hat \ell(s_i)\big).
\eqnum{8}
\]

Width without validity is meaningless, so the two must always be read together.
The practical consequence is the important one. Coverage estimated this way is
model-free, so it can convict an interval method of miscoverage without
sharing any of that method's assumptions. Section~\ref{sec:intervals-results}
shows a case where it does exactly that.

\section{Methods}
\label{sec:methods}

\subsection{The point predictor, derived}
\label{sec:predictor}

\subsubsection{Why a residual correction can help at all}

Write the base random forest \citep{breiman2001} as $\hat f$ and decompose the
truth as

\[
z(s) = \hat f(s) + r(s), \qquad r(s) := z(s) - \hat f(s).
\eqnum{9}
\]

The residual field $r$ is not noise: a forest fitted on a finite sample cannot
represent every relation, and whatever structure it missed remains in $r$. If
$r$ is predictable, a second model $\hat g$ carries information the map is
discarding, which is the logic of regression kriging \citep{hengl2018}.

\subsubsection{The shrinkage weight, and why it is not optional}
\label{sec:lambda}

Consider the corrected predictor $\hat y = \hat f + \lambda \hat g$ and its
population risk as a function of $\lambda$,

\[
\mathcal{R}(\lambda)
= \mathbb{E}\big[(r - \lambda \hat g)^2\big]
= \mathbb{E}[r^2] - 2\lambda\,\mathbb{E}[r\hat g] + \lambda^2\,\mathbb{E}[\hat g^2].
\eqnum{10}
\]

This is a convex parabola in $\lambda$. Differentiating and setting the
derivative to zero gives the optimum and the risk attained there,

\[
\lambda^\star = \frac{\mathbb{E}[r \hat g]}{\mathbb{E}[\hat g^2]},
\qquad
\mathcal{R}(\lambda^\star)
= \mathbb{E}[r^2] - \frac{\mathbb{E}[r\hat g]^2}{\mathbb{E}[\hat g^2]}
\;\le\; \mathbb{E}[r^2] = \mathcal{R}(0).
\eqnum{11}
\]

Three consequences follow, and each one motivates a design decision.

First, at the optimal $\lambda$ the correction can never hurt, and it helps
exactly to the extent that $\hat g$ correlates with the residual. That is the
reason a correction is worth attempting at all.

Second, $\lambda = 1$ is not the optimum. Adding the raw residual prediction is
optimal only when $\mathbb{E}[r\hat g] = \mathbb{E}[\hat g^2]$, that is only
when $\hat g$ is a perfectly calibrated predictor of $r$. A noisy $\hat g$ has
$\mathbb{E}[\hat g^2]$ inflated by its own variance, which pushes
$\lambda^\star$ below one. Shrinkage is therefore not a safety hack bolted on
afterwards; it is the solution of the risk minimisation.

Third, $\lambda^\star$ has to be estimated, and a badly estimated $\lambda$ can
hurt. Setting $\mathcal{R}(\lambda) > \mathcal{R}(0)$ in Eq.~(10)
and solving shows that the correction damages the map whenever

\[
\lambda > 2\lambda^\star .
\eqnum{12}
\]

Over-correction is a real failure mode rather than a hypothetical one, and
Sections~\ref{sec:crossfit} and \ref{sec:selection} exist to prevent it. In
practice we do not use the closed form of Eq.~(11), because it
presumes an $\hat g$ fixed in advance; $\lambda$ is selected on a grid jointly
with the choice of $\hat g$.

\subsubsection{Why the residuals must be cross-fitted}
\label{sec:crossfit}

Suppose everything were estimated on the same data: fit $\hat f$ on all $n$
points, form the in-sample residuals $\hat r_i = y_i - \hat f(x_i)$, fit
$\hat g$ to them, and choose $\lambda$ by minimising
$\sum_i (y_i - \hat f(x_i) - \lambda \hat g(x_i))^2$.

This fails, and it fails in a specific direction. A random forest interpolates
its training data to some degree, so $\hat f(x_i)$ is pulled toward $y_i$ and
the in-sample residual $\hat r_i$ is shrunk toward zero relative to the true
$r$. Worse, $\hat r_i$ is correlated with the noise in $y_i$ itself. A model
$\hat g$ fitted to those residuals partly relearns that noise, and when scored
on the same points it appears to explain more of $r$ than it can out of sample.
The selected $\lambda$ is therefore biased upward, which is exactly the regime
in Eq.~(12) where the correction damages the map.

The remedy is $K$-fold cross-fitting. Partition the calibration sample into
folds $F_1,\dots,F_K$, and for each $k$ fit the base model on $U \setminus F_k$
and predict $F_k$,

\[
\tilde f_i = \hat f^{(-k(i))}(x_i),
\qquad
\tilde r_i = y_i - \tilde f_i ,
\eqnum{13}
\]

and likewise fit each candidate correction on the training folds' residuals and
predict the held-out fold, $\tilde g_i = \hat g^{(-k(i))}(x_i, s_i)$. By
construction neither $\tilde f_i$ nor $\tilde g_i$ depends on $y_i$, so
$\tilde r_i$ is an honest residual and the criterion

\[
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(c,\lambda)
= \sqrt{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n}
\big(y_i - \tilde f_i - \lambda\,\tilde g_i^{(c)}\big)^2}
\eqnum{14}
\]

estimates the out-of-sample risk of configuration $(c,\lambda)$ rather than its
in-sample fit. This is the same device that underlies double or debiased
machine learning \citep{chernozhukov2018}: cross-fitting removes the
own-observation dependence that otherwise contaminates a plug-in estimate.

\subsubsection{Selection over a library, and the do-no-harm guarantee}
\label{sec:selection}

The correction is a library $\mathcal{C}$ of candidates, each able to recover a
different kind of structure the forest may have missed: a residual random
forest, a residual Cubist \citep{quinlan1992}, a single-hidden-layer network,
ordinary kriging of the residuals, a radial basis or kernel smoother, their
ensemble, and linear or quadratic recalibration of the base prediction.

We select

\[
(c^\star,\lambda^\star)
= \operatorname*{arg\,min}_{c \in \mathcal{C},\; \lambda \in \Lambda}
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(c,\lambda),
\eqnum{15}
\]

and the key structural detail is that the search space contains the null
correction. Since $\lambda = 0$ belongs to the grid $\Lambda$, and reproduces
the base forest exactly,

\[
\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(c^\star,\lambda^\star)
\;\le\; \widehat{\mathcal{R}}(\cdot\,,0)
= \widehat{\mathcal{R}}_{\mathrm{RF\ only}}
\eqnum{16}
\]

holds by construction. The method cannot select a configuration that is worse
than the plain forest on the out-of-fold criterion, and a correction is
deployed only once it has demonstrated an improvement on data it did not see.
This is the do-no-harm property, and it is why the estimator degrades to a
random forest, rather than to something worse, when the residual field carries
no recoverable signal.

Two qualifications belong here rather than in a footnote. The minimum of
$\widehat{\mathcal{R}}$ over many candidates is itself optimistically biased,
the familiar winner's curse, so
$\widehat{\mathcal{R}}(c^\star,\lambda^\star)$ is not an unbiased estimate of
the deployed model's risk and we never report it as one. That is precisely why
the two-stage design of Section~\ref{sec:designbased} matters: all selection
happens inside the calibration sample, and the reported accuracy is computed on
an independent probability sample that entered no part of fitting or selection.
The winner's curse contaminates the selection score, not the design-based
estimate.

\subsubsection{The spatial gate}
\label{sec:gate}

A residual correction is credible only where there are data to support it. Far
from every calibration point $\hat g$ extrapolates, and an extrapolated
correction adds variance without removing bias. We therefore attach a
location-dependent gate. Let

\[
d_1(s) = \min_{i \le n} \lVert s - s_i \rVert
\eqnum{17}
\]

be the distance from $s$ to the nearest calibration point, and set

\[
w(s) = \exp\!\big(-d_1(s)/\ell\big) \in (0,1],
\eqnum{18}
\]

so that $w \rightarrow 1$ where the correction is well supported and
$w \rightarrow 0$ where it is not. The deployed predictor is

\[
\hat y(s) = \hat f(s) + \lambda^\star\, w(s)\, \hat g^{(c^\star)}(s).
\eqnum{19}
\]

The length scale $\ell$ is not free. Beyond the range of the residual
variogram, residuals are effectively uncorrelated, so a spatially interpolated
correction carries no information about them and the gate should decay on that
scale. We therefore set $\ell = c_{\mathrm{gate}} \cdot \rho$ with $\rho$ the
fitted range, and select $c_{\mathrm{gate}}$ out of fold, including
$c_{\mathrm{gate}} = \infty$, which disables the gate, among the candidates.

\subsection{Geostatistics in the hyperparameter space}
\label{sec:geohyper}

Sections~\ref{sec:lambda} to \ref{sec:gate} already tune how much the
correction is trusted and how far it reaches. The variogram that feeds those
quantities, however, is normally a fixed recipe: a family chosen by habit, a
cutoff at half the maximum distance, a fixed number of lags, one estimator, and
a range consumed as a constant. For a method that argues geostatistical choices
should rest on evidence, that would be the last unexamined assumption. We
therefore place the variogram itself in the selection space.

\subsubsection{Family, and why the comparison is legitimate here}

Let $\hat\gamma(h)$ be the empirical variogram computed with the robust
estimator of \citet{cressie1980},

\[
2\hat\gamma(h) =
\frac{\Big(\frac{1}{|N(h)|}\sum_{N(h)} |r_i - r_j|^{1/2}\Big)^{4}}
{0.457 + 0.494/|N(h)| },
\eqnum{20}
\]

where $N(h)$ is the set of pairs separated by approximately $h$. Candidate
families, exponential, spherical, Gaussian and Mat\'ern
\citep{matheron1963,journel1978}, are each fitted to this same empirical
variogram by weighted least squares, and the family with the lowest weighted
residual sum of squares is retained.

Comparing fitted models by their sum of squares is legitimate in this direction
precisely because the binning and the pair counts are identical across
candidates, so the criteria are computed on the same quantity. It would not be
legitimate if the geometry or the binning changed, a point we return to below.

Parsimony is enforced among nested families. The Mat\'ern model nests the
exponential at smoothness $\kappa = 1/2$ and approaches the Gaussian as
$\kappa \rightarrow \infty$, and the smoothness is itself fitted, so raw sum of
squares hands Mat\'ern a win by one extra degree of freedom even when the truth
is exactly exponential, which we observed. We therefore retain the simplest
family whose criterion lies within a tolerance of the best.

\subsubsection{Range, binning and estimator}

The fitted range $\rho$ enters as a working scale rather than a constant. The
radial basis bandwidth is

\[
b = m_{b}\,\rho ,
\eqnum{21}
\]

with the multiplier $m_b$ selected out of fold. The value $m_b = 1/2$
reproduces the conventional derived value.

The lag binning, that is the cutoff fraction and the number of lags, and the
choice between the robust estimator of Eq.~(20) and the classical
estimator of \citet{matheron1963}, cannot be compared by variogram sum of
squares at all, because changing them changes the empirical variogram itself
and therefore the quantity being fitted. They are selected by out-of-fold
predictive merit through Eq.~(14), like every other
hyperparameter.

\subsubsection{Anisotropy}
\label{sec:aniso}

Nothing above requires the spatial structure to be isotropic, and assuming that
it is would sit badly with the argument of this paper. We estimate a geometric
anisotropy, a major-axis angle $\theta$ and a ratio
$\varrho = \ell_{\min}/\ell_{\max} \in (0,1]$, and map coordinates into the
space where Euclidean distance equals anisotropic distance,

\[
T_{\theta,\varrho}(s) =
\begin{pmatrix}
\sin\theta & \cos\theta \\[2pt]
-\cos\theta/\varrho & \sin\theta/\varrho
\end{pmatrix} s ,
\eqnum{22}
\]

so that $\varrho = 1$ is a pure rotation and recovers the isotropic method
exactly. The transform is applied to all three spatial components: the radial
basis, both query points and centres, so that the kernel becomes an ellipse
elongated along the major axis; the kernel residual smoother; and the ordinary
kriging correction, since kriging under geometric anisotropy is ordinary
kriging in the transformed space \citep{journel1978}.

Estimating $(\theta,\varrho)$ deserves a note, because the obvious routes fail.
Fitting one variogram per direction and taking the ratio of the smallest to the
largest fitted range is badly biased: the maximum of noisy range estimates
overshoots and the minimum undershoots, so the procedure reports anisotropy for
perfectly isotropic fields. Scoring candidate geometries by variogram fit in
the transformed space fails differently, because the transform changes the
distance distribution and hence the binning and pair counts, so the criteria
are not comparable. We therefore score each candidate geometry by $k$-fold
out-of-fold prediction error of a kernel smoother using the corresponding
distance, on the same held-out points for every candidate, with the bandwidth
set as a fraction of the median pairwise distance in the transformed space so
that the score responds to shape and not to the rescaling the transform
induces. On simulated fields this recovers the true angle and returns exact
isotropy for isotropic fields, and its errors are false negatives rather than
false positives.

The geometry is not imposed even once estimated. The anisotropic variant enters
the profile search as an additional candidate and has to beat the isotropic one
on the criterion of Eq.~(14).

\subsection{Design-based prediction intervals}
\label{sec:intervals}

\subsubsection{Construction}

Cross-fitting has already produced leakage-free residuals for the deployed
configuration,

\[
e_i = y_i - \big(\tilde f_i + \lambda^\star \tilde g_i^{(c^\star)}\big),
\eqnum{23}
\]

and these are exactly what split conformal prediction requires as conformity
scores \citep{papadopoulos2002,vovk2005,lei2018}. Define $q$ as the
$\lceil (n+1)(1-\alpha) \rceil$-th smallest value of
$|e_1|,\dots,|e_n|$ and attach to every cell the interval

\[
\big[\hat y(s) - q,\; \hat y(s) + q\big].
\eqnum{24}
\]

\subsubsection{Why it is valid, and what the validity rests on}

The conformal guarantee is a statement about exchangeability, not about
distributional form. If $|e_1|,\dots,|e_n|,|e_{\mathrm{new}}|$ are
exchangeable, then $|e_{\mathrm{new}}|$ is equally likely to occupy any rank
among the $n+1$ values, so

\[
\Pr\big(|e_{\mathrm{new}}| \le q\big) \;\ge\; 1-\alpha ,
\eqnum{25}
\]

with no assumption of Gaussianity, homoscedasticity or stationarity
\citep{vovk2005}. When calibration and target cells are both drawn by simple
random sampling from the same finite population, exchangeability holds, and the
marginal coverage statement of Eq.~(25) is a statement
about the population, which is to say it is design-based coverage in the sense
of Section~\ref{sec:picp}.

\subsubsection{Why it breaks under clustered sampling, and two repairs}

Now let the calibration sample be spatially clustered. The map still has to
predict everywhere, including regions far from every cluster, and the residuals
it will incur there are systematically larger than any residual observed within
the clusters, because those locations are extrapolations. Formally,
$|e_{\mathrm{new}}|$ for a distant target cell is not exchangeable with the
calibration scores, which are concentrated on well-supported locations. The
consequence is directional and predictable: $q$ is calibrated on residuals that
are too small, the intervals are too narrow, and coverage falls below nominal.
Section~\ref{sec:intervals-results} measures this, and the design-based
estimator of Section~\ref{sec:picp} is what makes the failure visible, since a
model-based coverage check would rely on the same exchangeability that has just
broken.

We consider two repairs. Covariate-shift weighting replaces the empirical
quantile by a weighted one with density-ratio weights
$w_i \propto p_{\mathrm{pop}}(x_i)/p_{\mathrm{sample}}(x_i)$, estimated by a
classifier separating calibration points from the known population covariates
\citep{tibshirani2019}. Its limitation is fundamental rather than technical:
reweighting can only redistribute mass over residuals that were actually
observed, and a clustered sample never observes the large extrapolation
residuals in the first place. Spatial-block conformal calibration instead
changes what the conformity scores are. The deployed configuration is refitted
on spatial $k$-means blocks and each held-out block is scored, so that every
residual is generated in an extrapolation regime resembling the one the map
faces \citep{roberts2017}. Those scores are larger, $q$ grows, and coverage is
restored. The price is wider intervals, and the gain is validity; an interval
that is narrow but invalid is worse than useless, because it is confidently
wrong.

All variants are compared against a quantile regression forest
\citep{meinshausen2006} interval, the standard model-based baseline, and every
interval is evaluated by design-based coverage and width through
Eqs.~(7) and (8).

\section{Materials and experimental design}
\label{sec:materials}

We use the public materials of \citet{wadoux2021}: above-ground live woody
biomass \citep{baccini2012} over a 928 by 1642 km Amazonian rectangle at 1 km
resolution, giving 1{,}310{,}143 cells, with 28 covariates known over the whole
grid. Calibration samples of $n=500$ are drawn under three designs, simple
random, systematic on a regular grid, and two-stage clustered random, and each
resulting map is evaluated design-based on an independent simple random
validation sample as in Section~\ref{sec:designbased}. We report 30 repetitions
for each design with a fixed seed.

Comparators are the untuned default random forest exactly as in the reference
study, a default Cubist, and a transparency model we label RF-tuned, which is
the base forest of the selected profile with no correction applied. RF-tuned
isolates the effect of tuning the forest from the effect of the residual
correction, and it matters for interpreting the result: if RF-tuned were to
account for the gain, the correction would be doing nothing.

Random forests are fitted with \texttt{ranger} \citep{wright2017} and all
variogram work uses \texttt{gstat} \citep{pebesma2004}. Every forest receives an
explicit seed, so the benchmark is reproducible. The estimator itself is
released as the \texttt{GeoVersa} package for R.

\subsection{Which geostatistical choices does the data make?}
\label{sec:choices}

Section~\ref{sec:geohyper} places the variogram family, its range, the lag
binning and estimator, and the anisotropic geometry in the selection space.
Table~\ref{tab:axes} reports what the data does with that freedom, because the
claim in the title stands or falls on it.

\begin{table}[!ht]
\centering
\caption{Number of repetitions, out of 30 per design, in which the out-of-fold
search departs from the conventional recipe, namely an exponential family, a
cutoff at half the maximum distance, 15 lags, the robust estimator, a bandwidth
of half the range, and isotropy.}
\label{tab:axes}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
Calibration design & Binning or estimator & Range scale & Anisotropy & Conventional recipe kept \\
\midrule
Simple random    &  9 & 7 & 4 & 10 \\
Regular grid     &  4 & 5 & 3 & 18 \\
Clustered random & 11 & 5 & 0 & 14 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

In two designs out of three the conventional recipe is displaced in a majority
of repetitions, so the defaults were not innocuous. Two contrasts within the
same design are worth drawing out. Under clustered sampling the anisotropic
geometry is accepted in none of the 30 repetitions, while the binning and
estimator are displaced in 11, the highest count of the three designs. Both
behaviours are what a design-aware procedure should show. A clustered sample
concentrates points in a few neighbourhoods and therefore carries almost no
information about direction at the scale of the domain, so the criterion
refuses a geometry it cannot estimate. The same clustering, however, severely
distorts the distribution of pairwise distances, so a cutoff at half the
maximum distance with 15 equal lags is a poor description of that sample and
the criterion replaces it. The mechanism is not a switch that turns
geostatistics on; it discriminates between the geostatistical parameters a
design can support and those it cannot.

Table~\ref{tab:modes} reports which corrector is selected, pooled over all 90
repetitions, and it is also the honest limit of the claim.

\begin{table}[!ht]
\centering
\caption{Selected corrector, pooled over 90 repetitions.}
\label{tab:modes}
\begin{tabular}{lr}
\toprule
Selected corrector & Count \\
\midrule
Residual random forest & 68 \\
Ensemble of residual forest, Cubist and kernel smoother & 15 \\
Ordinary kriging of residuals & 3 \\
Cubist delta & 2 \\
Kernel smoother & 1 \\

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Ordinary kriging wins outright in 3 of 90 repetitions, and a purely
geostatistical interpolator, kriging or the kernel smoother, in 4. In 68 the
selected corrector is a residual random forest which, carrying a shrinkage
weight, is formally a single boosting step. A reader is entitled to ask whether
this is geostatistics at all or a two-stage forest wearing geostatistical
clothes.

The answer is that the geostatistics is in the representation and the
regularisation rather than in the final estimator. The residual forest is not
fitted on coordinates alone. Its features include radial basis functions whose
centres and bandwidth come from the fitted variogram through
Eq.~(21), and from the anisotropic geometry of
Eq.~(22) when that is accepted, and its output is
multiplied by the spatial gate of Eq.~(18) whose length scale is the
fitted range. Remove the variogram and those features and that gate do not
exist. What Table~\ref{tab:modes} shows is that, once spatial structure has been
encoded this way, a forest exploits it better than a kriging predictor does on
these data, which is a statement about the relative strengths of two learners
rather than evidence that the geostatistical information was unnecessary. We
therefore make the narrower claim throughout: geostatistics supplies the
hypothesis space, the features and the hyperparameters, and the learner that
consumes them is chosen by the data.

\subsection{Robustness across validation estimands}
\label{sec:estimands}

Section~\ref{sec:point} scores the maps on a validation sample of $n=500$. Two
questions follow: does the advantage survive a much larger probability sample,
and does the ranking survive a change of scoring protocol? We drew 10 further
calibration samples under the simple random design and scored each under three
additional protocols, holding the configuration and the sample size fixed:
Population, an independent simple random sample of 20{,}000 cells drawn from
the population excluding the calibration cells; random 10-fold
cross-validation; and spatial block cross-validation with complete-linkage
blocks at 350 km.

Two qualifications bound what this experiment can show, and both belong before
the numbers. Only the first of the three is a map-accuracy estimand. The two
cross-validation protocols are not valid estimators of map accuracy, which is
precisely the result of \citet{wadoux2021} that we accept, and they are
reported here as model-comparison estimands only. Furthermore, the two
cross-validation protocols are aligned with the internal selection criteria of
the method, since corrections are accepted on random-fold out-of-fold risk and
the gate length is chosen by spatial cross-validation. They are therefore the
least independent of the four checks rather than the most adversarial, and
their differences should be read as an upper bound. The Population row carries
the evidential weight.

row is the 30-repetition campaign of Table~\ref{tab:accuracy}; the other three
share the same 10 calibration draws.}
\label{tab:estimands}
\begin{tabular}{lrrrrr}

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

DB-TARF wins all 10 draws under each additional protocol. Three cautions. The
one-sided paired Wilcoxon $p$ at 10 repetitions cannot fall below
$2^{-10} \approx 0.00098$, the value attained, so it certifies that all ten
draws favour DB-TARF and nothing stronger. These are 10 draws scored three ways
rather than 30 independent repetitions, and results within a draw are dependent
($r = 0.59$ between the two cross-validation differences). Finally the
cross-validation rows train on strict subsets, so their absolute values are not
commensurable with the design-based rows. We do not read the ordering of the
four differences as a gradient: adjacent protocols are not statistically
distinguishable ($p = 0.36$ between the two cross-validation protocols), and the
ordering is not one of spatial stress, since random 10-fold cross-validation is
the least spatially demanding of the four. Buffered leave-one-out
cross-validation was not run, requiring of order $10^3$ sequential fits per
repetition; it would be the most adversarial estimand available and is left to
future work.

\bottomrule

Under representative sampling, whether simple random or on a regular grid, the
plain conformal intervals are calibrated, with coverage of 0.896 and 0.907
against a nominal 0.90, and they are roughly 17 per cent narrower than the
quantile regression forest baseline, which over-covers at 0.937. Under
clustered sampling the plain intervals under-cover at 0.840, and the
covariate-shift weighting of \citet{tibshirani2019} barely helps, reaching
0.850. This is the limitation anticipated in Section~\ref{sec:intervals}:
reweighting can only redistribute mass over residuals that were actually
observed, and a clustered sample never observes the large extrapolation
residuals. The spatial-block calibration restores validity at 0.946, at the
cost of intervals that are about 40 per cent wider.

The result that matters most is not any single number but the fact that the
failure is visible at all. A model-based coverage check would have relied on
the same exchangeability assumption that clustering breaks, and would therefore
have reported the intervals as adequate. Estimating coverage as a population
parameter on a probability sample is what convicts them.

\section{Discussion and conclusions}
\label{sec:discussion}

The point result is a claim about where modelling decisions are made, not about
a new learner. Its ingredients are established, and what the experiments support
is narrower and more useful: when geostatistical decisions move into the
hyperparameter space and are validated out of fold, the map improves reliably
under the valid design-based yardstick, without the analyst judging a variogram
by eye or deciding in advance whether kriging is warranted. The RF-tuned control
localises the gain in the correction, Eq.~(9) bounds the downside by the forest
itself, and Table~\ref{tab:axes} shows the freedom is used and used
discriminatingly, the procedure refusing an anisotropic geometry under clustered
sampling while replacing the binning most often in exactly that design.

We are equally clear about the boundary. The selected corrector is usually a
residual forest and ordinary kriging wins outright in 3 of 90 repetitions. The
contribution is not that kriging has returned to the centre of digital soil
mapping, but that the geostatistical questions are posed explicitly and answered
by evidence, and that the answers change what the model does even when the final
corrector is a forest.

The uncertainty result is the more consequential contribution. Extending
design-based assessment from error to coverage is conceptually simple, three
lines in Section~\ref{sec:ht}, but it changes what can be checked. Intervals
that look reasonable can be badly miscalibrated for the population under
clustered sampling, the same regime in which \citet{wadoux2021} found
point-accuracy estimators to fail. The recommendation follows: assess coverage
design-based, and use a spatial-block calibration when clustering is suspected,
accepting wider but valid intervals.

\paragraph{Limitations} The point gain is incremental, of order 0.9 to 1.2
\Mgha{}. Opening the geostatistical hyperparameters leaves accuracy unchanged
relative to the fixed recipe, so the value demonstrated is that the choices are
made on evidence and are shown to matter, not that they lower error on these
data. Anisotropy is accepted in about one repetition in five under
representative sampling and never under clustering, and the model is geometric
and global, one angle and ratio for the whole domain. These results come from a
single, large and widely used dataset at one sample size, and generality remains
to be tested.

Map uncertainty, like map accuracy, must be assessed design-based, and interval
calibration must be made design-aware. Geostatistics, for its part, need not be
assumed into a map nor exiled from it: its parameters can be treated as
hyperparameters and asked to prove themselves, fold by fold. On these data the
corrector the model prefers is a forest, but it is a forest that sees the
landscape through a variogram.
